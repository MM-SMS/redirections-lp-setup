At 44, I Was Doing Everything Right — And Still Gaining Weight

For three years I fought my own body and lost. The problem was never willpower, and it was never my diet. It was a hormone that no one had thought to mention to me.

Rachel Mercer
Boise, Idaho  ·  9 min read

I want to be honest about how ordinary this story is, because I think that's the part that matters. I am not an athlete. I did not have a dramatic diagnosis. I was a 44-year-old woman with two kids, a job I mostly liked, and a body that had quietly stopped listening to me.

It started somewhere around 41. Nothing changed — that's what made it maddening. I ate the same food I'd eaten for a decade. I walked the same loop around the neighborhood. And yet the weight came anyway, about eleven pounds over two years, and all of it settled in one place: right around my middle, like a belt I couldn't take off.

I did what everyone does. I cut carbs. I cut wine. I went to a 5:45 a.m. bootcamp for eleven weeks and cried in the car twice. I bought a food scale and weighed my almonds. For two months I ate about 1,300 calories a day and I lost four pounds, and then I gained six back over Thanksgiving week — not from eating, from existing.

I wasn't failing at discipline. I had plenty of discipline. I was failing at something happening underneath it.

The 3 a.m. thing

Before the weight, there was the sleeping. Or rather, there wasn't.

I fell asleep fine. That was never the issue. The issue was that at some point between 2:40 and 3:20 in the morning, my eyes would open, and my heart would be going a little too fast, and my brain would hand me a completely random thing to panic about — an email I'd sent, a comment from a meeting in 2019 — and hold it there for ninety minutes. Then, right around the time it would have been reasonable to get up, I'd fall hard asleep and wake at 6:15 feeling like I'd been hit by a bus.

By 10 a.m. I needed coffee. By 3 p.m. I needed something sweet, urgently, not as a treat but the way you need water. I'd stand in front of the pantry at 3:15 every single afternoon and eat something standing up, and then feel genuinely confused about why I'd done it. I remember thinking: this isn't hunger. I don't know what this is, but it isn't hunger.

I went to my doctor. I described all of it. She ran a standard panel — thyroid, A1C, iron, the usual — and everything came back inside the normal range. She was kind about it. She said the words that I think a lot of women in their forties have heard: "Everything looks fine. It might just be your age. Try to manage your stress."

I left with nothing. And honestly, the "manage your stress" part made me angry, because I didn't feel stressed. My life was fine. I wasn't in crisis. That advice felt like being told to relax.

What a different doctor drew on a napkin

The thing that finally cracked it open wasn't an appointment. It was my sister-in-law's friend, an endocrinology nurse practitioner, at a backyard birthday party in July, who listened to me complain for about four minutes and then asked one question that stopped me cold:

"When you wake up at three in the morning — is your heart racing before you've thought of anything to worry about?"

Yes. Exactly that. Every time. The panic arrived first and my brain went looking for a reason afterward.

She took a napkin and drew two curves. The first one was what cortisol — the body's main stress hormone — is supposed to do across a day: a sharp rise about 30 minutes after you wake, which is what actually gets you out of bed, then a long steady slide down all day, bottoming out at night so you can sleep.

Then she drew mine. Flat in the morning — which is why I needed coffee to feel human — and then bumping up in the middle of the night, which is why I was waking at 3 a.m. with my heart going. The curve wasn't high everywhere. It was backwards.

The two curves she drew

A healthy cortisol rhythm peaks shortly after waking and falls all day. A dysregulated one is blunted in the morning and rises again overnight.

Healthy rhythm What mine looked like

Then she explained the part that reframed everything for me. Cortisol's job, biologically, is to make sure you have fuel available for an emergency. When it's chronically out of rhythm, your body does three things that are perfectly logical for surviving a famine and perfectly awful for a 44-year-old in Idaho: it pushes glucose into your blood, it prioritizes storing fat around the abdomen where it's fastest to mobilize, and it makes you crave quick sugar so you'll refuel.

That was my 3:15 pantry raid. That was the belt of weight around my middle that no amount of walking touched. That was the four pounds in two months on 1,300 calories.

The part I wish I'd known at 41

A standard blood panel doesn't catch this. My thyroid was fine, my A1C was fine, my iron was fine — and all of that was true. Cortisol rhythm isn't measured in a routine annual physical, so a woman can be told "everything looks normal" while the thing actually driving her symptoms was never on the test.

She was careful with me, and I'll be careful here too: she did not tell me there was a magic fix. What she said was that the rhythm responds to inputs, and that the highest-leverage input is the first hour after you wake up — because that's when the curve is set for the rest of the day. Light, protein, and getting the morning rise to actually happen.

She mentioned a handful of compounds that show up in the research on this — an adaptogenic root used in Ayurvedic medicine, an amino acid found in green tea, a phospholipid that seems to blunt the stress response, and a B-vitamin-like compound that gets studied a lot in women with insulin and hormone issues. She said if I wanted to try something, take it in the morning, take it daily, and give it six weeks before deciding anything.

So that's what I did. One glass, every morning, before anything else. It took me about forty seconds. I want to be clear that I changed nothing else on purpose — same food, same walk, same job, same kids.

What actually happened, in the order it happened

This surprised me: the scale was the last thing to move, not the first. Everything else went first, and I nearly quit at week two because I wasn't paying attention to the right things.

Week 1

Nothing. Honestly, nothing.

I felt slightly calmer in the mornings and assumed I was imagining it. If I'd been judging by the scale I would have stopped here.

Weeks 2–3

The 3 a.m. wake-ups stopped

Not gradually — it was more like I noticed on a Thursday that I couldn't remember the last time I'd seen 3 a.m. I was sleeping in one piece for the first time in years. That alone would have been worth it.

Week 4

The 3:15 pantry visit just… didn't happen

This is the one I still find strange. I didn't resist the craving. The craving stopped showing up. I'd get to 4 p.m. and realize I hadn't thought about food since lunch, which had not been true since my thirties.

Weeks 6–12

Then the weight moved on its own

Slowly, and from the middle first — my jeans before the scale. By twelve weeks I was down nine pounds without counting a single calorie. My husband's comment was that I'd stopped snapping at everyone at 6 p.m.

I'm sixteen months in now. I'm down the eleven pounds plus a bit. But if you asked me what I'd actually pay for, it wouldn't be the weight — it's that I sleep through the night and I don't have that low hum of dread in my chest at 4 p.m. anymore. The weight, it turns out, was the symptom. It was never the problem.
