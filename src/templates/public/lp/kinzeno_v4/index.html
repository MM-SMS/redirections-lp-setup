“My Leg Just Went Dead”: How One Ohio Grandfather Ended Three Years of Shooting Sciatic Pain

Frank D. was told to lose weight, stretch more, and learn to live with it. Then his right leg buckled while he was holding his eight-month-old grandson on a set of concrete porch steps. This is the story of what he did next — and what finally worked after everything else failed.

By Marcia Holt, Health & Wellness Desk · Updated August 25, 2026 · ✔ Fact-checked

The party was for his granddaughter’s fourth birthday. There were paper plates on the picnic table, a sheet cake with too much frosting, and eleven people in the backyard who all turned around at the same instant.

Frank was standing at the top of the porch steps holding Owen, his eight-month-old grandson, when his right leg simply stopped being there.

“It wasn’t pain, exactly. Pain I was used to,” he says. “It was like somebody unplugged the leg. One second I’m standing, the next second the knee is folding and I’m going down — and I’ve got the baby.”

His daughter was fifteen feet away and screaming. Frank got his left forearm hooked around the porch rail, twisted his shoulder into the post, and pulled Owen hard against his chest as he went down onto one hip on the concrete. The baby never touched the ground. Frank sat there on the step, shaking, unable to make words come out.

Nobody sang happy birthday that afternoon.

“That was the third time in two months my leg had given out without any warning at all. But it was the first time I had someone in my arms.”

Frank D., 63

It Started With a Twinge He Ignored for a Year

Frank spent thirty-one years as a machinist and the last decade building furniture in a garage shop he’d fitted out himself. He and his wife Linda have been married 34 years. Three kids, four grandkids, a dog that needs walking twice a day.

The first sign was so small he doesn’t remember the date. A dull ache in the left side of his lower back after a long day at the bench. He stretched. It went away.

Then it started travelling.

“It moved into the buttock, then down the back of the thigh, then the calf. Always the same leg. And it changed character — it stopped being an ache and turned into this hot electrical thing. Like touching a live wire from your hip to your heel.”

Within a year, ordinary life had quietly rearranged itself around one leg. He stopped sitting at the dinner table because twenty minutes in a chair set the whole leg on fire. He stopped driving anywhere further than the grocery store. He started sleeping in the recliner, then stopped really sleeping at all.

The mornings were the part he still doesn’t like talking about. Getting his legs over the side of the bed took a full negotiation — roll, brace, breathe, count to three. Bending forward to reach his own feet was out of the question for the first hour of every day.

And then there was the cough.

“Nobody warns you about that one. You cough, or sneeze, or God forbid you laugh hard at something, and it fires a bolt straight down the leg. I started bracing myself before I coughed. That’s a strange thing to catch yourself doing.”

What Was Actually Happening Inside His Back

It took eighteen months and four appointments before someone finally put a name to it.

The sciatic nerve is the largest nerve in the human body — roughly the thickness of a finger where it leaves the lower spine. It runs from the lumbar spine, through the buttock, down the back of the thigh, and branches off below the knee all the way into the foot. When something presses on it — a bulging disc, a narrowed opening between vertebrae, or a deep hip muscle locked in spasm — the nerve reports it the only way it knows how: as pain, fired down the entire length of the leg.

That’s why sciatica behaves so differently from ordinary back pain. The problem is in the back. The alarm goes off in the leg.

The four signs doctors look for

  • Shooting pain down one leg. Sharp and electric rather than dull — often described as a jolt or a hot wire running from the lower back through the buttock and down toward the foot. It usually affects one side only.
  • Numbness, tingling or pins and needles. Patches of the calf, ankle or foot that feel asleep, buzzing, or oddly distant when touched.
  • Weakness in the foot or the whole leg. A foot that catches on carpet edges, a knee that folds without warning, difficulty pushing off when climbing stairs.
  • Pain that gets worse when you sit or cough. Sitting increases pressure on the lower spine; coughing, sneezing or straining raises it sharply for an instant — and the nerve answers immediately.

What surprised Frank most was how ordinary his case turned out to be. Commonly cited estimates suggest that up to 40% of people will experience sciatic pain at some point, and that it peaks between the ages of 45 and 64 — exactly the years when most people are still working, still lifting grandchildren, and least able to spend a decade sitting down.

“That was the part that made me angry, honestly,” Frank says. “I’d spent two years thinking I’d done something to myself. Turns out half the men my age at the hardware store are walking around with some version of it, and every single one of them has been told the same thing I was: take something for it and try to stay off your feet.”

Frank had all four. The specialist described a chronically irritated nerve root, complicated by a deep hip muscle — the piriformis — that had been clenched for so long it had become part of the problem itself.

“He drew it for me on a piece of paper,” Frank says. “The muscle sits right on top of the nerve, back behind the hip joint. It goes into spasm, it squeezes the nerve, the nerve screams, so you tense up and guard the leg — which makes the muscle tighter. Round and round. He said the fire that started this thing was long out. What was left was the alarm, still ringing.”

“The nerve is the alarm. The muscle clamped around it is what keeps pressing the button. Get that muscle to let go and you give the nerve room to calm down.”

The Things He Stopped Doing

The garage went first.

Frank had built that shop over eleven years — a table saw he’d saved two winters for, a wall of clamps, a bench he made himself out of hard maple. Standing at that bench had been the way he ended almost every day.

“There’s a dresser out there for my youngest granddaughter that I started in the spring and never finished. Three drawers done. The fourth one is still sitting in the clamps. Every time I opened that door and looked at it I felt like I was looking at a headstone.”

Then the fishing trip with his son, cancelled two years running — not because of the fishing, but because of the four-hour drive up and the thought of sitting in a truck seat that long. Then the church pancake breakfast, because of the folding chairs. Then his own anniversary dinner, cut short at the restaurant because he couldn’t stay in the booth.

“Nobody has a big conversation about it,” Linda says. “You just stop being invited to things, because everybody knows the answer already.”

Three Years, Thousands of Dollars, and Nothing to Show for It

What follows is a list Frank can recite from memory, because he lived every line of it.

Prescription pills

They took the edge off for a few hours at a time. They also gave him a burning stomach, a permanent fog, and the uneasy feeling of a man reading about long-term kidney effects at two in the morning. The moment they wore off, the leg came back — sometimes louder than before.

Every cream on the pharmacy shelf

Menthol rubs, capsaicin creams, the ones that smell like a hospital corridor. “They all do the identical trick,” he says. “They make the skin go hot or cold so your brain has something else to think about. You slap it on, wipe your hand on your jeans and walk off smelling like a locker room. Twenty minutes later the buzz fades and the tight knot behind the hip is still exactly where you left it. Nobody ever told me how to actually use the stuff.”

Steroid injections

Two weeks of genuine relief after the first one. Frank remembers telling Linda it was over. The pain came back with interest, and the second injection did less than the first.

Physical therapy, rest, and an inversion table

The therapy helped while he was on the table and faded by the time he got to the car. The inversion table went into the garage after a month. And “rest,” the advice he got most often, turned out to be its own trap — every week in the recliner made the leg weaker and the next attempt at walking worse.

By the spring of last year Frank had spent, by his own accounting, north of four thousand dollars. He had a garage full of unfinished furniture, a wife who had started carrying the groceries in alone, and a specialist who used the phrase “manage expectations.”

Two nights after the birthday party, Linda found him sitting on the edge of the bed at 3 a.m. with both hands wrapped around his thigh.

“She didn’t say anything. She just sat down next to me. And in the morning she started looking — not at pills, not at another specialist. She remembered what the specialist said about the muscle, and she wanted something gentle we could use every single night, at home, right on that spot.”

It took her about a week to find it.
