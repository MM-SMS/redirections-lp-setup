I Lost My Wallet Two Hours Before My Daughter's Rehearsal Dinner. My Speech Was Folded Inside It.

For thirty years my family has charged me what they call “the Ray Tax.” In Austin last spring, the bill finally came due at the worst possible moment.

As told by “Ray,” 61, retired shop teacher, Lakewood, Colorado
6 minute read

My wife Diane keeps a running total. Not on paper, she's too kind for that, but she can recite it at Thanksgiving if somebody gets her started. She calls it the Ray Tax: every dollar this family has spent replacing something I set down and walked away from.

I taught high school wood shop for thirty-one years. I can tell you where every chisel in that room hung. I could find a 3/8 Forstner bit in the dark. But hand me my own wallet, and within the week it will be on the roof of the truck, in the produce aisle, or in a coat I donated.

If you are one of us, you know the routine. The pocket pat. The second pocket pat, slower, as if the first one might have been lying. The cold drop in your stomach. Then the phone calls: the bank, the other bank, the place you ate lunch, the place you only think you ate lunch.

Believe me, I tried everything

People who don't lose things always have advice for people who do. I have taken all of it.

The bowl by the door. Diane bought a nice ceramic one. Keys and wallet go in the bowl, every time, no exceptions. It works beautifully on the days I come home through the front door. I come home through the garage.

The bright wallet. My son's idea: swap the brown leather for safety orange so I'd spot it anywhere. I felt like a crossing guard for six weeks, and then I left it on a gas pump in Golden, where I assume it is still visible from space.

The chain. A buddy from the district gave me a trucker wallet with a chain that clips to a belt loop. It did keep the wallet attached to my jeans. It did not help on the days I wore different jeans.

The checklist. Phone, wallet, keys, glasses, said out loud at every doorway. Diane says I sound like a man boarding a submarine. It is the only method that has ever worked, and it works right up until I'm distracted, tired, or happy. Which is to say it fails at exactly the moments that matter.

That's the part the organized people never understand. You don't lose things because you don't care. You lose them because your mind is on something bigger than a pocket. A grandkid's ballgame. A doctor's phone call. Your daughter's wedding.

So I had made my peace with it. I figured it was just the price of being me.

Then Emily got engaged

Our daughter was getting married in Austin, where she and her fiancé live. Outdoor ceremony, live oaks, a rehearsal dinner on Friday night where the father of the bride was expected to stand up and say something worth hearing.

I am not a speech guy. So I did what a shop teacher does: I drafted it, measured it, cut it down, and wrote the final version by hand on a single index card. Both sides. Three weeks of work. I folded it once and tucked it behind my driver's license, because that was the one place I knew I would not forget to bring.

I do see the irony now.

Diane watched me like a hawk through security in Denver. Wallet into the bin, wallet out of the bin, wallet into the pocket. She actually said “good job,” the way you would to a seven-year-old. I let it go. It was a happy weekend.

We landed in Austin a little after two. Picked up the bags, ordered a ride, and I sat in the back with a garment bag across my knees for the twenty-five minutes to the hotel. I remember pulling my wallet out to show the driver a photo of Emily in her dress. I remember him saying she looked like her mother, which was the right answer.

4:05 p.m., hotel front desk

“I'll just need a card and a photo ID, Mr. Kowalski.”

Pocket pat. Second pocket pat. Jacket. Garment bag. Diane's purse, for reasons I can't explain.

Nothing.

My license. Every card we own. About two hundred dollars in cash. And one index card, both sides, that I had exactly zero copies of. The rehearsal dinner started at six.

Diane did not say a word. After thirty-four years of marriage, that is the scariest sound I know.

The car was long gone. I could not remember the driver's name, and the ride was booked on Diane's phone, which was at four percent. The young man at the desk was very polite about it. He offered to hold the room. He offered me a bottle of water. He did not have a spare father-of-the-bride speech behind the counter.

I ran the list in my head. Cancel the cards, which means an hour on hold. No license, which means I am not getting on the plane home Sunday without a long conversation at security. No cash for the tip jar at my own daughter's rehearsal dinner. And the toast. I tried to say the first line to myself and got as far as “Emily, when you were four.” After that, sawdust.

I stood there in my travel clothes doing the math on whether a man can give a speech from memory when he can't remember where he put a wallet eleven minutes ago.

And then, in my shirt pocket, my own phone buzzed.

I stared at it for a good five seconds before I remembered what it was.

What I'd forgotten was in there

Back on Father's Day, Emily had slipped a gold “credit card” into my wallet.

She handed it to me with a look I know from her mother. “Dad. I'm not getting married while you're on hold with the bank. Put this behind your license and forget about it.”

So I did both. It looked like one more piece of plastic among the others. It is not a credit card. It is a tracker called Ondrela, about as thin as two credit cards stacked together, and it had been riding around in my back pocket for ten months without my giving it a second thought.

The next forty minutes

I tapped the notification. A map opened with a little wallet icon sitting on East Riverside Drive, a couple of miles from the hotel. A minute later it refreshed and the icon had hopped a few blocks north.

Here is what I understand now and did not understand then. The card has no GPS antenna of its own. It sends out a quiet Bluetooth signal, and any nearby phone on Apple's Find My network (or Google's Find Hub network, if you're on Android) can pick that signal up and pass the location along, anonymously and encrypted. In the middle of Austin on a Friday afternoon, there are a great many phones. My wallet was getting noticed at every red light.

Diane's phone had just enough battery to open the ride app and message the driver: Wallet in your back seat, we can see roughly where you are, any chance you can swing back? He wrote back that he'd looked and didn't see anything, but he was ten minutes away and happy to come by.

When his car pulled up, the map said my wallet was right there. He was right, though: nothing on the seat, nothing on the floor. So I pressed the button I had never once pressed, the one marked Play Sound.

A sharp little chirp started up from somewhere down by the door. The wallet had slid into the gap between the seat cushion and the door frame, standing on edge, where neither of us would have found it by looking. I fished it out with two fingers. License, cards, cash, and one slightly bent index card.

I gave the toast at 7:15. Emily cried. Diane cried. I'd like the record to show that I did not lose anything else that weekend.

What this card actually is

When I got home I read up on the thing properly, because I do not like owning tools I can't explain. Here is the plain version.

Thin enough to forget

About 1.7 mm thick and the size of a bank card, so it sits in a regular card slot. No fob, no bulge.

Works with the phone you have

It pairs with the tracking app already built into iPhone (Find My) or Android (Find Hub). No extra app to learn, and setup takes about a minute.

Left-behind alerts

Walk away without the item and your phone tells you. That one notification is what saved my weekend.

It rings

A built-in alarm, listed at around 90 dB, that you trigger from your phone when you're within Bluetooth range. Made for couch cushions and car seats.

Rechargeable, wirelessly

The maker says up to 6 months per charge. When it runs low, you set it on a standard Qi wireless charging pad.

No monthly fee

You buy the card once. There is no subscription, and location data is encrypted through the phone network it uses.

Where the family has put theirs

After Austin, Diane ordered more. Nobody had to talk her into it.

The fair warning section

I spent three decades telling teenagers what a tool can and cannot do before they switched it on. Same courtesy here.

What it does well

  • Disappears into a wallet, passport sleeve or luggage pocket
  • Works for both iPhone and Android households
  • Warns you when you walk away from the item
  • Rings so you can find it in a car, couch or coat
  • No subscription; wireless recharging
  • 60-day return policy listed on the official site

What it doesn't do

  • It is not live GPS. You see the most recent spot where a nearby phone detected it
  • Fewer phones around means fewer updates, so rural areas are weaker
  • A checked bag in an aircraft hold won't update until it's near phones again
  • Metal or RFID-blocking wallets can shorten its range
  • The battery is sealed in; after some years the card itself gets replaced
  • No tracker can promise you'll get a lost item back

One more thing, and I mean it: this is for your own stuff. Apple and Google phones are designed to warn people when an unknown tracker is traveling with them, and secretly tracking another person is against the law in many states. Put it in your wallet. Leave everybody else's alone.

Questions people ask me

Do I need to pay for an app or a plan?

No. It uses the tracking app that comes with your phone (Find My on iPhone, Find Hub on Android). There's no monthly fee.

Can I watch it move on the map in real time?

Not like a car GPS. The location refreshes whenever a phone on the network passes near the card. In a city that can be every few minutes. Out in the country it can be a lot longer.

How do I charge it?

Lay it on a Qi wireless charging pad. The manufacturer states up to six months of use per charge; your mileage will depend on how often it rings and reports.

Is it waterproof?

The maker describes it as water resistant and built for daily use. No official IP rating is published, so I'd treat that as “fine in the rain,” not “fine in the pool.”

What if I don't like it?

The official site lists a 60-day return policy. Read the returns page there for the exact conditions before you order.
