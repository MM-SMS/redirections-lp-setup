Part 01

The sound that emptied my bedroom

I never heard it. That is the strange part. For years I went to bed convinced I slept like a stone, and every morning I woke up feeling like I had been hit by a truck.

My wife Dana heard it. In the spring of 2014 she recorded three minutes and forty seconds of it on her phone and played it back to me at breakfast. I genuinely thought she was playing me a video of furniture being dragged across a floor upstairs. Sawing. Rattling. Then a long, awful pause, and then the whole thing starting again.

She had been lying awake next to that for years. She stopped telling me about it because I got defensive every time she did. So one night she took her pillow and went into the guest room, and she said what everyone says: just for tonight.

Just for tonight lasted eleven years.

What I understood far too late is that snoring is not only a noise problem. It is a sleep problem, and it was mine as much as hers. I was falling asleep at red lights. I read the same paragraph of a work document four times and could not tell you what it said. I snapped at my kids over nothing and then hated myself in the car afterwards. I put it down to being in my forties. I put it down to stress, to coffee, to the mattress. I put it down to everything except the actual cause.

I wasn't tired. I was hollowed out. Eleven years of sleep I never actually got.

Part 02

Everything I bought that did not work

I want to list these, because if you are reading this you have probably bought some of them too, and I want you to know it was not you being stupid.

  • The chin strap. Two weeks. My jaw ached from the moment I woke up until lunchtime, and I still snored, only now with my mouth clamped shut.
  • The boil-and-bite mouthpiece. I gagged on it. When I finally slept through a night with it in, I woke with my bottom teeth aching and a slab of wet plastic on the pillow.
  • Mouth tape. Small red welts on my cheeks for a month. Dana said it made me look like I had been in an accident.
  • Nasal strips and nose clips. These are the ones that really got me, because they seemed so obvious. They did absolutely nothing.
  • The wedge pillow, the tennis ball in the pyjama shirt, the humidifier, the two apps. The apps at least gave me data. The data said I was still snoring.

That last sentence is the whole point, and it took a physician to say it to me plainly. Straps hold your jaw. Tape holds your lips. Clips hold your nostrils open. Not one of them touches the tissue that is actually making the noise.

Part 03

The part nobody had explained to me

My doctor sent me to a sleep clinic to rule out apnea. I did not have it. What I had, the clinician told me, was ordinary, mechanical, non-apneic snoring, and she drew it on the back of a form.

For most people the sound is not made in the nose at all. Up to eighty per cent of it starts further back, in the soft tissue of the mouth and the throat. When that tissue is inflamed and swollen, and when mucus has thickened over it during the day, the airway you breathe through at night narrows. Air has to squeeze. Squeezing air makes soft tissue flutter. Fluttering tissue is the noise.

Now hold that next to the drawer of gadgets. A nose clip widens a nostril that was never the bottleneck. A strap changes the angle of a jaw. None of it reduces the swelling, and none of it clears the mucus. They were all answers to a question I did not have.

Which raised an obvious question. If the problem is inflammation and mucus in the throat, why is nobody putting anything in the throat?

Five nights

I did not tell Dana I had bought it. I want to be honest about why: I had been wrong so many times that I did not want another conversation about it. I just used it and went to sleep in my own room with the app running.

Night one. Dana came in at six in the morning holding her phone, which is not a thing that happens in our house. She had heard something at about two and had woken up properly, because the something was silence. My app had logged forty-one seconds of snoring across seven hours. My baseline was routinely over an hour and a half.

Night two. Fifty seconds, all of it in one stretch after I had eaten late.

Night three. Nothing the app was willing to call snoring.

Night five. Dana moved her pillow back.

I have read enough since to know I was on the faster end of it, and that the sensible advice is to give it five consecutive nights before you judge anything. But I am not going to pretend the first morning was not the single best morning of my adult life.

Many people notice a difference on the very first night. The manufacturer asks you to use it for five consecutive nights before you judge it, because inflammation takes a few days to settle properly. I had my first quiet night immediately, but I kept going for the full five.

No, and it does not claim to. It is for ordinary, non-apneic snoring. If you stop breathing in your sleep, gasp awake, or are exhausted all day despite sleeping a full night, see a doctor and get tested. I did exactly that before I bought anything.

There is no sedative in it. It does not put you to sleep, it takes the obstruction out of the way so the sleep you were already having actually works. Waking up clear-headed was the first thing I noticed.
