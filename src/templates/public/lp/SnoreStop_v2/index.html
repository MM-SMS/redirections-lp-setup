I hadn’t remembered a single dream in six years. Then I found out why.

I called it insomnia. My doctor called it stress. My husband called it “the chainsaw.” It took a sleep clinic, a drawer full of gadgets and one small bottle on my nightstand to figure out what was really waking me up at 3:14 every single night.

I want to start with the dream, because the dream is the reason I’m writing this at all.

On a Tuesday in September I woke up at 6:40 in the morning — not at 3:14, not at 4:02, at 6:40, with daylight already coming through the blinds — and I was crying. Not sad crying. I had been dreaming about my grandmother’s kitchen in Dayton. The yellow linoleum, the radio on the windowsill, the smell of her coffee. It was so vivid that for a few seconds I genuinely did not know where I was.

I lay there and tried to remember the last time I had woken up from a dream. I couldn’t. I went back through my journals later that week and the honest answer was somewhere around 2020. Six years of nights with nothing in them except the ceiling and that red clock.

This blog started as a place to vent about not sleeping. A lot of you found it because you’re awake at 3 a.m. too. So I owe you the full story of what finally changed — including the parts I’m embarrassed about.

The 3:14 club

If you’ve never had it, here’s what “insomnia” looked like for me. Falling asleep was never the problem. I’m a middle-school librarian with two teenagers; I was out cold by 10:30. The problem was what came after.

Somewhere between 2 and 4 a.m. I’d jolt awake. Dry mouth, sore throat, heart going, a vague feeling that something had startled me. I’d check the clock — it was so often 3:14 that my sister started calling it “Pi o’clock” — and then I’d lie there for an hour, sometimes two, running through every email and every bill and every dumb thing I said in 2009.

Sleep diary — Tues, Feb 14

Asleep 10:20. Awake 3:14. Again 4:50. Up at 6.

Felt like I’d been hit by a bus. Forgot Ava’s dentist appt. Cried in the car in the school parking lot. Valentine’s Day. Great.

Total sleep: ~5 hrs?? (again)

The days were worse than the nights. I’d re-read the same paragraph four times. I drank coffee at 3 p.m. and then wondered why I couldn’t sleep. I got short with the kids over nothing. I put on fourteen pounds I couldn’t explain. I told myself it was being in my forties, it was hormones, it was screens, it was stress. Everybody I asked had an answer and every answer was a little bit true and none of them fixed anything.

What nobody asked me — not one doctor, not one article — was whether I snored.

The recording

My husband Tom is the kind of sleeper who could nap on a runway. So it took a very particular night for him to say something: a Saturday in March, his mom staying with us, and his mom asking at breakfast, very politely, whether we “had a problem with the furnace.”

It was me. Three minutes and fifty-two seconds of rattling, sawing, a long terrible silence, a snort — and then, every time, the sound of me rolling over. Tom said that pattern happened all night.

And that’s when I went back to my sleep-tracking app and actually looked at it, instead of just looking at the score.

Fourteen awakenings. Most of them I had no memory of. My “3:14” was just the one that finally stuck. And look how little time I spent in REM — the stage where dreams happen. Of course I wasn’t dreaming. I was never staying asleep long enough to get there.

I didn’t have insomnia. I was waking myself up, over and over, with my own throat.

What the sleep doctor drew on a napkin

I asked my GP for a referral and did a sleep study. I’ll be honest, I was terrified it would be sleep apnea and I’d be sleeping in a mask for the rest of my life. It wasn’t apnea — please, if you stop breathing or gasp awake, get tested; that’s a different thing and it matters. What I had was what the specialist called ordinary, “primary” snoring, with something called arousals: tiny micro-wake-ups, dozens a night, each one triggered by the airway narrowing.

She drew it for me on the back of an intake form, and I’ve redrawn it here because it changed how I thought about everything:

“So what do I do about it?” I asked. She listed the usual things: lose a little weight, sleep on your side, cut the wine, try an oral appliance. So, being me, I went home and bought everything.

The drawer

The chin strap made me look like I’d had dental surgery and I still snored, just with my mouth closed. The mouthguard made me gag, and I woke up with it on the pillow and my jaw clicking. Mouth tape left red marks on my face that the kids at school asked about. Nasal strips and a nose clip did absolutely nothing — which made perfect sense once I looked at that napkin drawing again.

Every single thing in that drawer worked on the outside of the problem. Hold the jaw. Hold the lips. Prop the nostrils. None of it touched the swollen tissue at the back of my throat that was actually making the noise.

Sound familiar? There’s a reason the gadgets don’t work.
